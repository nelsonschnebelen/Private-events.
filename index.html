The season calendar

The whole year turns on the next six weeks.

Corporate holiday decisions land between now and early October — long before the parties happen. Miss this window and you're competing for the leftovers in November, at triple the ad cost.

September
You are here

Peak intent. The dates everyone wants — the second and third weeks of December, Thursdays and Fridays — are being claimed this month. Mid-size companies book 10–12 weeks out, which lands exactly here.

October
Last real call

Final wave of mid-size bookings, plus every small team working on a 6–8 week runway. Ad costs are still reasonable. This is the last affordable month to prospect.

November
Expensive

Ad costs spike with retail. What's left to sell is overflow: weeknight dates, lunches, smaller rooms. Harvest your warm audience instead of prospecting.

December
Execute

The room runs. Your only job now is the send-off — and the rebooking card that puts next year's date on the calendar before they leave.

January
Cheapest month

Ad costs collapse after the holidays. Sell Q1 kickoffs, off-peak dinners and January "we couldn't get a December date" parties — and lock next December's rebookings.

What bookers told us

Why planners pick someone else.

A holiday party planner is shopping three to five venues at once, usually on a deadline handed to them by someone senior. The patterns in what makes them choose — and ghost — are remarkably consistent.

The fastest clear answer wins

Inquiries go out in batches. The venue that replies first with complete information — not "let's hop on a call" — takes the deposit. In season, most venues take days. Beat them with hours.

Fix → the 2-hour proposal
Hidden fees kill the contract

A $95/guest quote that becomes $128 after service charge, tax, and fees is the top reason deals stall — and an office manager spending company money cannot go back for more twice.

Fix → all-in instant quote
Nobody wants to repeat themselves

Being bounced from host stand to manager to chef — re-explaining the party each time — is the most-cited frustration in event booking. One named captain, first call to final toast.

Fix → one point of contact
"Inquire for pricing" is a dead end

Vague capacity and pricing behind a form makes planners bounce to the venue that published the number. They need a figure to take to their boss today, not after a site visit.

Fix → everything on the page

The standard

The Booker's Bill of Rights — publish it and mean it.

Every one of these answers a frustration planners named out loud. Print them on your events page. Almost none of your competitors will match them in season.

A complete proposal within 2 hours, in business hours

Not an acknowledgment. Not "let's find time to chat." The menu, the room, the all-in price, the date held for 72 hours.

The all-in price, first

Service charge, tax, and any peak-date premium in every number we show you. The quote you see is the contract you sign.

One captain, start to finish

You'll have a name and a direct line. You will never re-explain your party to a second person.

Dietary needs handled by default

Vegan, gluten-free, allergies, kosher-style — flagged once on the form, confirmed on the menu card, no side conversations.

Plain-language deposit & a real weather clause

25% holds the date. Full transfer to a new date with 14 days' notice, or if the city shuts down for snow. No fine print you need a lawyer for.

The ad strategy

Buy attention in the month they're deciding — not the month they're partying.

Q4 advertising costs climb 20–50% as retail floods the auction, peaking around Black Friday. But holiday party intent is peaking right now, in September. That gap is the entire opportunity — and it closes at the end of October.

You have about six weeks of affordable auction left. Intent is at its peak right now while cost is still near its floor. Three phases, one budget.

Starting in September means you've missed the leisurely warm-up — there's no month left to build an audience before you need it. So run warming and converting in parallel, and get 80% of the season's spend out the door before November.

Four angles, because creative burns out fast.

One ad will fatigue in three weeks at season spend. Build four distinct concepts up front, each with a few cuts, and rotate them as frequency climbs.

The office manager

Make them the hero

"You planned it. We'll make you look like a genius."

The person booking is rarely the person paying — they're being judged on it. Sell the credit they get, not the canapés.

The CFO's proxy

Lead with the real number

"$110 a head. All in. No surprise line items."

Budget-approval anxiety is the biggest silent objection in corporate booking. Killing it in the headline earns the click from every planner who's been burned.

The procrastinator

Name the date that's left

"Three December Thursdays left. Then we're done."

Genuine scarcity, stated specifically. Update the number weekly — vague urgency reads as marketing, a real date reads as inventory.

Last year's client

Assume the rebooking

"Same room, same night, one year later. Shall we hold it?"

Your cheapest bookings of the season. A single retargeting campaign against last year's guest list will usually outperform everything else you run.
